SD Standard

Welcome to the SD Standard documentation.

The Sustainable Design Standard helps designers apply sustainability principles throughout the design process and assess the lasting impacts designers or a project has on people and the planet.

The Standard is organized into four pillars:

  • Environment: natural resources and ecological impact
  • Society: social equity and community well-being
  • Culture: traditions and identity
  • Finance: economic viability and long-term value.

The SD Standard criteria apply to visual communication projects (editorial design, exhibit design, signage, packaging, web design, interaction design, and motion graphics). Other emerging practices will be considered on an ongoing basis.

Criteria can inform communication departments addressing sustainability or corporate responsibility and align with the UN Sustainable Development Goals, as indicated below each criterion.

Contributions to the SD Standard, and criteria are welcome.

Overview

The purpose of the SD Standard is to give designers of communications, packaging, and products around the world a tool to help them assess the impact their work has environmentally, socially, culturally and financially. When a project begins, you input the parameters of your project for assessment. (These can be saved as “project” if you have paid a fee.) Your intended targets will be kept in your project file. Once the project is complete, simply confirm that you’ve met the criteria and you will be provided a number that can be posted or printed on the object itself.

Scoring Philosophy

The scoring component of the SD Standard criteria can be applied in different ways depending on the purpose of the assessment, the level of evidence available, and how the design entity or designer wishes to report alignment with the criteria.

The SD Standard is intended to support both improvement and accountability. For this reason, scoring should distinguish between criteria that have already been addressed, criteria that are partially addressed, criteria that are supported by evidence, and criteria that represent future or modelled improvements.

The following scoring structures are provided as reference models.

Option A: Criteria addressed / not addressed

In the simplest scoring structure, each criterion is scored as either addressed or not addressed.

If a criterion is met to the best knowledge of the design entity or designer, 1 point may be assigned to that criterion. If the criterion is not met, or if there is not enough information to determine whether it has been met, 0 points are assigned.

This approach can be useful for quick scans, early-stage assessments, or project teams that want a simple way to identify strengths and gaps.

A pass logic may also be applied by pillar. For example, a project may be considered to have passed a given pillar if it addresses more than 50% of the criteria in that pillar.

0 = not addressed
1 = addressed

Example reporting:

Environmental criteria addressed: 12/24
Social criteria addressed: 8/17
Cultural criteria addressed: 4/8
Financial criteria addressed: 3/7

Option B: Pass / partial / fail / verified

A more detailed scoring structure can be used when the assessment needs to reflect different levels of progress, documentation, or evidence.

In this model, each selected criterion is scored based on the degree to which it has been addressed and the strength of the supporting documentation.

0 = not addressed
1 = partially addressed
2 = fully addressed
3 = verified with evidence

This approach is useful when a project has a mix of completed actions, partial actions, and actions that can be independently supported by documentation, supplier records, certifications, measurements, or other forms of evidence.

Reporting can also separate actual results from hypothetical or modelled improvements. For example:

Baseline: 18/90 points
Improved scenario: 62/90 points
Verified current impact: 24/90 points
Modelled additional impact: +38 points

This avoids mixing achieved results with projected or hypothetical outcomes. It also allows a project team to show both current performance and improvement potential.

Option C: Weighted scoring

Some assessments may choose to apply different weights to different criteria.

For example, an organization may decide that certain criteria are especially important because they align with its mission, sector, geography, stakeholders, material impacts, or sustainability priorities. In that case, priority criteria may be assigned a higher point value, while other criteria may retain a lower point value.

Standard criterion: 1 point
Priority criterion: 5 points

Weighted scoring can help an assessment reflect the relative importance of different sustainability issues. However, weighting should be defined clearly before the assessment begins, and the rationale for assigning higher weights should be documented.

For transparency, any weighted score should be reported alongside the weighting method used.

Recommended reporting principles

Regardless of the scoring structure used, SD Standard reporting should be clear about:

  • which criteria were included in the assessment
  • whether the assessment applies to a project, a design entity, or both
  • whether each score is self-assessed, documented, or independently verified
  • whether reported results are achieved, estimated, or modelled
  • what evidence, assumptions, or limitations support the score

This distinction is important because a score can represent different levels of confidence. A self-assessed score can help a team improve its design process, while an evidence-based or verified score can support stronger public claims.

The scoring method should therefore be selected based on the purpose of the assessment. A quick project scan may only require a simple addressed/not addressed score. A public impact report, recognition pathway, or certification process may require a more detailed scoring model with documentation, evidence, and verification.

Contributing

Thank you for your interest in contributing to the SD Standard.

The SD Standard is a living and evolving framework built on collaboration. It is designed to grow through contributions from designers, researchers, developers, educators, sustainability practitioners, suppliers, accessibility experts, students, and organizations working toward more responsible design practices.

This project includes more than code. Contributions may include improvements to the criteria, Knowledge Base, methodology, scoring models, tools, data structures, case studies, translations, project documentation, design assets, and technical infrastructure.

About the SD Standard

The SD Standard helps designers reduce environmental harm, improve social outcomes, support cultural sustainability, and strengthen financial resilience through better design decisions.

The framework is organized around four pillars:

  • Environment: natural resources, emissions, waste, energy, water, materials, and ecological impact
  • Society: accessibility, labor, health, safety, equity, human rights, education, and public benefit
  • Culture: local relevance, Indigenous knowledge, endangered languages, cultural diversity, and community participation
  • Finance: fair compensation, economic benefit, accountability, transparency, and long-term value

The SD Standard is intended to support both practical design decisions and more transparent ways to evaluate, document, and improve the impact of communication design projects.

Who can contribute

Contributions are welcome from people with different types of knowledge and experience, including:

  • designers, UX designers, developers, writers, editors, and translators

GitHub workflow

For most contributions, please use the following workflow:

  1. Check existing issues and pull requests to see whether the change has already been suggested.
  2. Open an issue if you want to propose a new feature, major content change, methodology change, or technical improvement.
  3. Fork the repository.
  4. Create a new branch for your contribution.
  5. Make your changes.
  6. Test the project locally, if applicable.
  7. Submit a pull request with a clear description of what changed and why.

Pull requests should be focused and limited in scope whenever possible. A smaller pull request is usually easier to review than one that changes many unrelated parts of the project.

Pull request guidelines

When submitting a pull request, please include:

  • a short summary of the change
  • the reason for the change
  • any related issue numbers
  • screenshots or screen recordings for visual changes, when helpful
  • notes about testing, if applicable
  • sources or references for content or methodology changes
  • any known limitations or follow-up work

For content, research, or methodology changes, please explain the reasoning behind the proposed update. For technical changes, please describe how the change was tested.

All contributions should support the SD Standard’s purpose: helping designers reduce environmental harm, improve social outcomes, support cultural sustainability, and strengthen financial resilience.

Contributions should aim to be:

The SD Standard is not intended to be static. Criteria, scoring methods, guidance, tools, and examples may evolve as new research, technologies, regulations, and design practices emerge.

E1: Environmental Impact Reduction

Display ID: E1
Pillar: Environmental Criteria
Points: 1
Mandatory for Certification: No
Category: Environmental Impact Reduction and Transparency
Applicability: Project
Project types: Print, Digital, Hybrid
Related SDGs: SDG 11, SDG 12, SDG 13

Summary

Reduces environmental impact and is supported by a qualified life cycle study (LCS) or quantitative life cycle assessment (LCA).

Criteria

The project strategy includes at least one action to reduce environmental impact that is supported by a qualified life cycle study (LCS) or quantitative life cycle assessment (LCA).

Why it matters

Actions lead to designs with a lower impact.

Extended guidance

What is an Environmental Impact Reduction strategy?

This should include a description of the environmental impact and reduction target, and how it is to be accomplished and measured. For example: the design brief for a packaging project may list the goal of reducing the environmental impact associated with virgin fiber by using recycled paper fiber. The design brief specifies that no less than 50% of the project’s paper be made of recycled paper, as measured by weight.

Some environmental impacts, linked to design outcomes include:

  • Reduction of Material and Resource Use,
  • Reduction of Energy (in sourcing, manufacturing, delivery, use, disposal, etc.),
  • Material Substitution (toward materials and energy sources that have demonstrably lower impacts),
  • Localization of Materials and Energy (that decrease ecological impacts), Increasing the Likelihood of Recycling

What are environmental impacts?

Environmental impacts refer to the potential environmental consequences of a particular action, activity and/or project. The environmental impact may be positive or negative, but the term is most often used when characterizing a negative result. Environmental impacts are often described in terms of particular categories of impact. Typical impact categories include:

  • Climate Change or Global Warming Potential
  • Ozone Layer Depletion
  • Air Pollution
  • Water Pollution
  • Land Degradation
  • Resource Depletion
  • Acid Rain
  • Eutrophication
  • Species Extinction
  • Solid Waste

How to apply this criterion

Describe how the project reduces one of the environmental impact.

E2: Product Ingredients Transparency

Display ID: E2
Pillar: Environmental Criteria
Points: 1
Mandatory for Certification: No
Category: Environmental Impact Reduction and Transparency
Applicability: Project
Project types: Print, Hybrid, Packaging
Related SDGs: SDG 12

Summary

Displays ingredients or material components.

Criteria

The project includes a list of all components and/or ingredients.

Why it matters

The display of “ingredients” or material components.

Extended guidance

What ingredients and/or materials should be listed?

An ingredients list, or list of the material contents of the product, should provide enough information so that the user is informed of the materials that comprise the product and product packaging. In cases where trace amounts of materials may be present and difficult to identify, every effort should be made to identify the ingredient. To provide for such transparency in materials, this information may be listed on the product itself and/or on the product’s packaging.

How detailed should this ingredients list be?

The list should name all ingredients contained in the final product, but need not provide the chemical formula. In the case of proprietary materials, the material or chemical category may be listed. For example, in the case of a product that contains a proprietary bio-plastic material, it may be listed as ‘polylactic acid,’ which describes the chemical category.

What is meant by ‘transparency?’

Transparency refers to the goal of honest and open communication and exchange. It supports all pillars of sustainability. For this Criterion, a product ingredients list discloses all material contents or energy inputs, which are now ‘transparent’ to the consumer.

E3: Ink, Printing, and Finishes

Display ID: E3
Pillar: Environmental Criteria
Points: 1
Mandatory for Certification: No
Category: Environmental Impact Reduction and Transparency
Applicability: Project
Project types: Print, Hybrid, Book, Postcard, Packaging, Signage, Exhibit, Campaign
Related SDGs: SDG 12

Summary

Reduces ink coverage on printed materials.

Criteria

The project reduces or eliminates the use of inks, varnishes and finishes, or requires no printing.

Why it matters

Reduces ink coverage on printed materials.

Extended guidance

Why should attention be paid to ink coverage?

Ink coverage is directly related to material consumption, as well as waste generation and disposal. Therefore ink reduction can have a positive impact on resources, energy and pollution.

What is ink?

For the purpose of this criterion, ink is a material that contains pigments or dyes, used to mark a surface with color, text and/or image. Ink is typically ‘printed’ on paper, or other paper-like material using letterpress, offset lithography or silk-screening. Ink may also used to print on hard surfaces such as glass bottles, cans, and board.

Are soy-based inks considered to be environment-friendly?

‘Soy-based’ inks often contain petroleum-based carriers, and/or the soy may be sourced from farms that displaced rain forests (deforestation).

Are there other inks that are considered environment-friendly?

Printing presses that are Cradle-to-Cradle certified (bronze, silver, gold), use inks composed of identifiable chemicals that can be absorbed naturally in a biological cycle, unlike petroleum-based inks.

Other options may include natural earth and mineral pigments.

Summary

Why it matters

Reduces or eliminates toxins in a product.

Extended guidance

What is a toxin?

A toxin is generally defined as a substance that has the potential to damage or kill. The response depends upon dose (concentration), exposure pathway and assimilation, and may vary from organism to organism.

Are there toxins that I should be aware of?

Both the US Environmental Protection Agency and the UN Environment Programme maintain active lists of known toxins. The European Commission has Directives on Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) and Restriction in Hazardous Substances in Electrical and Electronic Equipment (RoHS).

How do I learn if there are toxins potentially present in the product I am designing?

A life cycle study will list potential toxins. An understanding of the supply chain will indicate potential areas where toxins may be present.

Where can I read the declaration and what it’s about?

The Universal Declaration on Animal Welfare (UDAW) is a proposed inter-governmental agreement to recognise that animals are sentient, to prevent cruelty and reduce suffering, and to promote standards on the welfare of animals such as farm animals, companion animals, animals in scientific research, draught animals, wildlife and animals in recreation. What is a carbon offset credit?

‘Carbon offsets’ refer to activities that reduce greenhouse gases, such as renewable energy, forestry, and resource conservation projects. Participation in carbon offset projects may be purchased as ‘carbon credits.’ Businesses and individuals may use carbon offset credits as a means of reducing and managing their greenhouse gas emissions. For example, an individual or a company may purchase 3rd party certified carbon credits to ‘offset’ travel- and/or energy-related greenhouse gas emissions.

See: http://www.nrdc.org/globalwarming/offsets.asp

What are greenhouse gas emissions?

Greenhouse gas emissions refer to the seven (7) man-made [anthropogenic] gases known to contribute to the greenhouse gas layer. These are carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), nitrous oxide (N2O), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), sulfur hexafluoride [SF6], and nitrogen trifluoride (NF3). Avoiding and reducing emissions are mandated by the Kyoto Protocol.

See: http://unfccc.int/kyoto_protocol/items/2830.php

What is third-party certified?

Organizations that sell carbon offset credits have their carbon offset projects verified by a ‘3rd party certified’ entity. That is, a ‘3rd party’ that is independent of the organization offering the carbon offsets. In addition, this 3rd party has been accredited to perform the certification. Companies such as ClimeCo and TerraPass are examples of organizations that sell carbon credits and which are 3rd party certified.

What is air pollution?

Air pollution may be described as the degradation of air quality caused by pollutants. Greenhouse gas emissions are considered to be air pollutants. In addition, Ground Level Ozone (O3), Carbon Monoxide (CO), Sulfur Dioxide (SO2), Lead, and Particulate Matter are pollutants that contribute to poor air quality.

See: https://www.who.int/health-topics/air-pollution

What is Creative Commons?

A Creative Commons (CC) license is one of several public copyright licenses that enable the free distribution of an otherwise copyrighted work. CC provides an author flexibility (for example, they might choose to allow only non-commercial uses of their own work) and protects the people who use or redistribute an author’s work from concerns of copyright infringement as long as they abide by the conditions that are specified in the license by which the author distributes the work. Extended guidance

Economic Benefits recognizes that a design project can create value beyond the immediate transaction between the client and the designing entity. In the SD Standard, this criterion asks whether the project strategy identifies and creates economic benefits for other people, organizations, communities, suppliers, regions, or ecosystems of work.

A project may meet this criterion when it intentionally supports broader economic value, such as strengthening local suppliers, creating paid opportunities for collaborators, improving access to useful tools or knowledge, supporting community-owned initiatives, helping small organizations become more financially resilient, or contributing to local/regional economic development.

This criterion is especially important because sustainable design should not only reduce harm; it should also help build healthier systems of exchange. F2: Profitability

Display ID: F2
Pillar: Financial Criteria
Points: 1
Mandatory for Certification: No
Category: Basis of Financial Sustainability
Applicability: Project
Project types: Print, Digital, Hybrid
Related SDGs: SDG 8, SDG 10

Summary

Ensures the design entity is paid for work performed.

Criteria

The project budget or contract financially compensates the design entity.

Why it matters

The designing entity profits from the project.

Extended guidance

Profitability recognizes that sustainable design work must also be financially sustainable for the designing entity. A project should not depend on unpaid overtime, underpricing, hidden labour, or financial loss in order to be completed. In the SD Standard, this criterion asks whether the project budget or contract allows the designer, studio, agency, or design team to complete the work while generating a profit.

This criterion is important because design practices that routinely absorb extra hours, unpaid revisions, rushed production, or underestimated project management time can become unsustainable. Even when a project has strong environmental or social value, it should not place the financial burden of that impact entirely on the designers or creative workers producing it.

A project may meet this criterion when there is evidence that the project was planned, priced, and managed in a way that protects the financial health of the designing entity. This could include a clear project budget, estimated hours, defined scope of work, revision limits, payment milestones, and an agreement for how additional work will be billed.

How to measure this criterion

Profitability can be measured by comparing the original project estimate with the final project outcome. Useful indicators include:

  1. Final profit after project completion The project generated a positive financial result after all project-related costs were accounted for, including labour, subcontractors, materials, software, printing, travel, taxes, admin time, and other direct expenses.

  2. Estimated hours vs. actual hours The number of hours used to complete the project did not significantly exceed the number of hours assigned to the project. This helps show whether the project was accurately scoped and managed.

  3. Effective hourly rate The final fee, divided by the actual number of hours worked, still meets or exceeds the designing entity’s minimum sustainable hourly rate.

  4. Scope control The project contract included clear limits on deliverables, revisions, meetings, and timelines. Work outside the approved scope was treated as additional billable work.

  5. Extra-hour clause or change-order process The contract included a clause stating that once the assigned project hours are used, additional work is charged at an agreed hourly rate or through a new estimate. This helps prevent hidden unpaid labour.

  6. Payment structure The project included deposits, milestones, or scheduled payments that reduced financial risk and supported cash flow during the project.

A pass condition could be:

This criterion is met when the project budget or contract does not assume financial loss by the designing entity, and when final project records show that the project generated profit or maintained a sustainable effective hourly rate after all labour and direct costs were considered.

F5: Accountability and Transparency

Display ID: F5
Pillar: Financial Criteria
Points: 1

Project types: Print, Digital, Hybrid

Summary

Adheres to transparent financial management and documentation practices.

Criteria

Adheres to transparent financial management and documentation practices.

Why it matters

Adheres to international accounting standards.

Extended guidance

Accountability and Transparency recognizes that sustainable design requires clear, responsible, and trustworthy financial and project management practices. In the SD Standard, this criterion asks whether the designing entity and the client adhere to accountable and transparent processes and procedures.

This criterion is important because sustainability claims, project decisions, budgets, timelines, and responsibilities should be visible and understandable to the people involved. A project is more financially sustainable when decisions are documented, communication is consistent, accounting is responsible, and all parties understand how resources are being used.

A project may meet this criterion when the designing entity follows recognized accounting practices, works with a certified accountant or qualified financial professional, keeps accurate financial records, and uses clear contracts, invoices, budgets, and payment schedules. It may also be supported by broader organizational practices, such as publishing an annual sustainability report, sharing relevant policies, or documenting how the organization manages its environmental, social, cultural, and financial responsibilities.

This criterion can also apply to project communication. The designing entity should keep the client, collaborators, suppliers, and other relevant stakeholders informed about the project’s status, key decisions, budget implications, delays, risks, approvals, and changes in scope. Transparency is not only about final reporting; it is also about maintaining trust throughout the process.

How to measure this criterion

  1. Recognized accounting practices are used The designing entity follows appropriate national or international accounting standards, keeps organized financial records, and uses clear invoicing, payment, and expense-tracking systems.

  2. Qualified financial support is in place The designing entity works with a certified accountant, bookkeeper, or qualified financial professional, especially when managing larger, multi-partner, grant-funded, public-interest, or complex projects.

  3. Clear contracts and budgets exist The project includes a written agreement, defined scope, payment terms, budget expectations, change-order process, and responsibilities for both the client and the designing entity.

  4. Project updates are shared consistently All relevant parties are kept informed about the different stages of the project, including milestones, approvals, delays, risks, scope changes, budget changes, and final delivery.

  5. Sustainability or impact reporting is available The designing entity publishes, or contributes to, an annual sustainability report, impact summary, public policy, or other transparent documentation of its practices and progress.

  6. Decisions are documented Important project decisions, approvals, changes, and responsibilities are recorded in a way that can be reviewed later if needed.

A pass condition could be:

This criterion is met when the designing entity uses accountable financial and project management procedures, maintains clear documentation, and communicates project status, responsibilities, and changes transparently with relevant parties.

Display ID: F6

Why it matters

Continuous improvement in the designing entity’s organization.

Extended guidance

Standards Improvement recognizes that sustainable design is not only about the outcome of a single project, but also about what the designing entity learns and improves because of the project. In the SD Standard, this criterion asks whether the project resulted in improvements to the designing entity’s documented standard procedures.

This criterion is important because many sustainability gains become more meaningful when they are repeated. A project may reveal a better supplier, a more efficient workflow, a lower-impact production method, a clearer accessibility checklist, a stronger contract clause, or a more inclusive research process. When those lessons are documented and added to the organization’s regular way of working, the impact can extend beyond one project and improve future work.

A project may meet this criterion when it leads to a specific update in the designing entity’s internal standards, templates, checklists, policies, onboarding materials, project management processes, quality-control steps, or evaluation methods. The improvement should be documented enough that it can be reused by the same team or shared with future collaborators.

How to measure this criterion

  1. A documented procedure was updated The project resulted in a change to an internal process, checklist, template, brief, contract, production workflow, accessibility review, supplier selection process, or sustainability evaluation method.

  2. The improvement is reusable The change is not only a one-time project decision. It becomes part of the designing entity’s standard way of working and can be applied to future projects.

  3. The reason for the change is clear The team documented why the procedure was improved, such as reducing waste, improving accessibility, protecting workers, increasing transparency, reducing project risk, or improving financial sustainability.

  4. The change is connected to evidence or project learning The improvement is based on something learned during the project: a measured result, client feedback, supplier information, production issue, accessibility audit, post-project review, or team reflection.

  5. The procedure is accessible to the team The updated process is stored somewhere the team can realistically use it, such as a shared folder, project management system, internal wiki, onboarding guide, design system, or operations manual.

Lifecycle Assessment

Definition

A method for evaluating the environmental impacts of a product, service, or system across its life cycle.

Why it matters

It helps teams identify where material, energy, emissions, and end-of-life impacts can be reduced.

Print on Demand

Definition

Print on demand (POD) is a printing technology and business process in which book copies (or other documents, packaging, or materials) are not printed until the company receives an order, allowing prints in single or small quantities.

Why it matters

Reduces unnecessary material waste.

Related criteria

Sustainability

Definition

In 1987, the United Nations Brundtland Commission defined sustainability as “meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.”

Why it matters

Sustainability is important to making sure that we have and will continue to have, the water, materials, and resources to protect human health and our environment.

Related criteria

  • E1: Environmental Impact Reduction

Sustainable Materials

Definition

Materials chosen to reduce environmental harm through renewability, recyclability, or lower lifecycle impact.

Why it matters

Material choice often dominates environmental impact in physical design outputs.
