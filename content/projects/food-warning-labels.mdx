The warning label system follows a similar approach to tobacco warning labels, giving buyers an immediate, high-contrast message that is hard to overlook, before purchase. In doing so, it makes hidden health risks easier to recognize, simplifying the need to read a more complex nutrition guide table, making these more accessible to a wider population.


These graphics have been in use in Latin America, starting in Chile in 2012, and rolled out in other countries, help educate people about cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and certain types of cancer, leading causes of death in the Region, all linked to unhealthy diets and the high consumption of ultra-processed and processed foods with high levels of sugars, fats, and sodium.

From an SD Standard perspective, this project shows how visual communication can improve health and safety, reduce information inequality, and support more accountable product design. Its strength is not only the graphic form of the black octagon, resembling a stop sign, but the larger system behind it: nutrition thresholds, policy and regulatory enforcement, public education, and packaging redesign.

The project also demonstrates a “better project pattern” for designers: when communication affects public well-being, clarity should take priority over persuasion. A successful package is not only one that sells the product, but one that helps people understand the consequences of choosing it.
